## Claims by topic

### Paywall layout & content order

- Recommended paywall content order, top to bottom: hero image/video, then benefit copy, then pricing options, then the purchase button, then legal links (terms/restore). [S10]
- Plan-count guidance: a single plan is simplest but limits experimentation; two plans need clear price contrast to work; three plans give room to anchor and match different user segments. [S10]
- A radically simplified single-page paywall (image, headline, CTA) beat a detailed feature-comparison-chart paywall, 111% higher conversion. [S16]
- Simplifying the CTA button from a plan-specific label to a single word ("Continue") improved conversion 10% over plan-name CTAs. [S16]
- A feature comparison table (free vs. pro) on a paywall lifts both trial starts (+18%) and paid conversion (+15%); 52% of top 100 apps use one. [S34]

### Trial framing & billing reminders

- A trial-timeline design that states trial length, the cancel-by date, and promises a reminder before billing increased trial signups by 23%. [S5]
- The same redesign made users opt in to the pre-billing reminder notification far more often (6% → 74% opt-in). [S5]
- Telling users up front that they'll get a reminder before being charged cut related support complaints by 55%. [S5]
- Superwall's built-in free-trial reminder defaults to firing one day before the trial ends (`beforeTrialEndDays: 1`), and the lead time is configurable per paywall. [S8]
- Practitioner rationale for reminder-before-billing: warning users before the charge lets uninterested users cancel cleanly instead of disputing the charge afterward, which protects trial-conversion numbers and avoids chargebacks. [S9]
- A recommended trial-ending risk-reversal message pattern is a plain-language reminder on the last trial day rather than a silent auto-charge. [S21]
- Cal AI's paywall reassures users with prominent "No Payment Due Now" copy placed near the CTA, alongside "Cancel anytime" microcopy. [S12]
- Practitioner pattern: let users toggle the free trial on/off themselves rather than always defaulting them into it, as a testable paywall variant. [S11]
- Adding a short free trial to a weekly plan sharply increases that plan's lifetime value versus the same plan with no trial: $7.40 → $54.50 (+636%). [S7]
- Longer free trials convert better, peaking in the 17–32 day range (45.7% median trial-to-paid conversion). [S3]
- Trials longer than 4 days show conversion rates exceeding 60% for top-quartile apps. [S15]
- Roughly 3 in 10 annual subscribers cancel within their first month, which argues for setting trial/billing expectations clearly up front. [S3]

### Plan count, default selection & price anchoring

- Two-plan paywalls are the dominant layout across app categories (41–60% of paywalls show exactly two plans). [S2][S31]
- Single-plan paywalls are a category outlier and multi-plan adoption varies by vertical: Shopping shows 40% single-plan, Health & Fitness shows 60% multi-plan. [S2]
- Annual plans appear on 28–44% of paywalls by category; lifetime plans are a Gaming-specific pattern (18% of paywalls). [S2]
- Offering more pricing options increases conversion with diminishing returns: 2 products beat 1 by 61%, and 3 products beat 2 by 44%, across 32M+ paywall interactions. [S14]
- Among paywall A/B test categories, changing the number of plans shown is the single highest-win-rate lever for lifetime value (57.1% win rate). [S7]
- One documented high-converting price-anchoring setup pairs a monthly plan with a cheap weekly plan as the anchor (monthly $18.99 anchored against weekly $5.99). [S7]
- Price-anchoring practice: price the monthly plan deliberately high — not to sell it, but to make the annual plan look cheap by comparison. [S12]
- Cheap annual plans retain subscribers far better than expensive monthly plans over a year (36.0% vs 6.7% one-year retention). [S3]
- Preselecting the default plan is one of the strongest levers on which plan people buy, but the cheapest-looking default can win conversion while reducing total revenue. [S36]
- Price A/B tests rarely move conversion; most price tests fail to win (28.3% win rate). [S7]
- Only a small minority of apps use promotional/discount offers on their paywall at all (9.3%). [S4]

### Social proof & testimonials

- Specific, large user-count and star-rating social proof (rather than vague claims) is used on high-revenue app paywalls: Speak cites "5 million users" and a 4.8-star rating from 140,000+ reviews, alongside Flo and YAZIO using testimonials/ratings. [S12]
- In Japan/Asia-market paywalls, social proof, testimonials and trust indicators are treated as essential because users look for third-party validation before purchasing. [S15]
- A recurring carousel/"stories"-style hero pattern (auto-advancing through plan benefits or reviews) appears on production paywalls, observed in ROI (paging benefits carousel) and Pestle (auto-advancing "story lines"). [S22]
- Social-proof (testimonial/rating) tests move conversion more for unknown brands than for well-known ones (successful lift 5–15%). [S32]
- Leading a food/diet app's paywall with a 5-star review plus a specific outcome stat ("86% of users improved their diet") as social proof raised trial conversion 72%. [S6]
- Redesigning a driver-license-prep app's paywall with a trial toggle and real App Store reviews as social proof raised ARPU 17.02%. [S6]

### Risk reversal & CTA copy

- "No commitment, cancel anytime" style risk-reversal copy is treated as a low-cost, broadly-applied addition to paywalls — described as "nearly effortless" and consistently used. [S16]
- Superwall's own recommended CTA-copy test set for paywalls is "Start free trial" vs. "Continue" vs. "Try [product] free for 7 days" (a suggested test, not a reported result). [S12]
- CTA copy and offer-framing tests produce smaller but compounding gains (successful lift 2–10%). [S32]

### Personalization from onboarding

- Adding a single onboarding question that builds credibility (e.g., "How did you hear about us?" with options like doctor/TV/friend/scientific article) before the paywall raised conversion for a health app by roughly 10%. [S20]
- A two-person team built a fitness/calorie app with an extensive personalized onboarding flow (25+ cards) before its paywall and scaled it to 15M+ downloads and $30M+ ARR in under two years before acquisition (Cal AI, acquired by MyFitnessPal). [S20]
- Redesigning onboarding (trimmed copy, clearer value prop) ahead of the paywall, without personalizing per-answer, drove large revenue gains despite flat/declining installs: ARPU +102%, total revenue +50% over 4 months, activation revenue +414%. [S18]
- Running dozens of paywall A/B tests (packaging, billing cycle, intro offers, win-back) without engineering support doubled revenue over a year (42 tests, 2x revenue in 12 months). [S19]
- Headline / value-proposition changes are one of the higher-leverage single-element paywall tests (successful lift 5–20%). [S32]

### Video, carousel & multi-page paywalls

- Multi-page (multi-step) onboarding paywalls outconvert single-page paywalls: 12.41% vs 9.07% conversion (a 37% relative lift), across 40M+ onboarding paywall opens. [S13]
- Adding video to a paywall drove a large revenue increase for a fitness app (Fitness AI: ~80% revenue increase after adding paywall video). [S17]
- Moving a paywall to before onboarding and adding video to it sharply increased both reach and conversion for the same app: +50% paywall views, install-to-trial conversion doubled. [S33]

### Paywall timing & placement

- 82% of free-trial starts happen the same day a user first opens the app, so the paywall functions as part of onboarding. [S1][S3]
- 90% of free-trial starts and 44.5% of all purchases happen on the day of install (Day 0), reinforcing that the paywall is an onboarding-moment decision. [S7]
- 80% of purchases happen the first time a user reaches the paywall after onboarding, arguing for showing the paywall early rather than deep in the app. [S10]
- Practitioner guidance: show every user a paywall within the first 12–24 hours after install, since that window sees the strongest conversion intent. [S11]
- Onboarding-placed paywalls that include a free trial convert best of any placement (1.35% average conversion). [S7]
- Onboarding-placed paywalls can be a major ARPU driver: one app's onboarding paywall redesign raised ARPU 60% in 5 months and now drives about half of its trial starts (Mojo). [S15]
- Adding an in-app upgrade button/CTA surfaced revenue that wasn't cannibalized by existing purchase flows (+10–20% revenue, Avast). [S15]
- Reframing a crypto/fintech app's pricing with a visual "price chart" discount narrative raised conversion 2.7% → 3.24% (+20% relative). [S6]

### Conversion benchmarks: hard paywalls vs. freemium

- Hard paywalls convert far better than freemium at Day 35: 10.7% vs 2.1% median trial-to-paid conversion, per the State of Subscription Apps 2026 data. [S1][S4]
- A different, 2025-edition benchmark of the same metric reported a different split — 12.11% (hard paywall) vs 2.18% (freemium) median Day-35 conversion — noted here as a conflicting figure because it is from a different annual report, not a correction of the 2026 figure above. [S3]
- Hard paywalls generate far more revenue per install than freemium at Day 60 (8x: RPI $3.09 vs $0.38). [S1]
- One-year subscriber retention is nearly identical for freemium and hard-paywall apps despite the conversion gap (28% freemium vs 27% hard paywall). [S1]
- Only a small fraction of downloads convert to paying subscribers within the first month industry-wide (1.7% within 30 days). [S15]
- Revenue concentration among top apps is extreme and widening: the top 5% of new apps earn over 400x the bottom 25% in year one ($8,880 vs $19). [S3]

### A/B test levers ranked by impact

- Win-rate ranking of monetization test types by LTV uplift: localization highest (62.3% win rate), then trial structure (59.6%), plan duration (58.7%), price (45.5%), visual/copy lowest (34.6%). [S7]
- Offer/plan-matrix changes (price + trial + plan mix changed together) are the single biggest paywall lever (10–40% range, "sometimes much more"). [S32]
- Delaying the close ("X") button for a few seconds after the paywall appears is a recognized practitioner tactic to increase read-through and subscription likelihood; no published number quantifies its effect in isolation. [S35]

### Apple App Store rules

- Any content/functionality unlocked within an app (e.g., subscriptions) must be sold via Apple's In-App Purchase, not an outside mechanism: "you must use in-app purchase." [S23]
- Apps may not use license keys, QR codes, crypto wallets, or similar workarounds to unlock paid content instead of IAP: "Apps may not use their own mechanisms to unlock content or functionality." [S23]
- Loot-box-style randomized purchases must disclose item odds to the customer before purchase: "disclose the odds of receiving each type of item to customers prior to purchase." [S23]
- Developers can apply for an entitlement to link out to their own website to purchase digital content/services outside IAP: "may apply for entitlements to provide a link in their app." [S23]
- In the US storefront, apps do not need that entitlement to include external purchase buttons/links/calls to action: "not required for developers to include buttons, external links, or other calls to action." [S23]
- Apple permits auto-renewable subscriptions in any app category: "Apps may offer auto-renewable in-app purchase subscriptions, regardless of category." [S23]
- An auto-renewable subscription must provide ongoing value, last at least 7 days, and work across all the user's devices: "the subscription period must last at least seven days and be available across." [S23]
- Before asking someone to subscribe, the app must clearly state what they get for the price: "you should clearly describe what the user will get for the price." [S23]
- In a subscription purchase screen, the total amount to be billed must be the single most visually prominent price shown: "amount that will be billed must be the most prominent pricing element." [S24]
- Any discount/savings comparison price must be shown smaller and subordinate to the actual billed price: "should be displayed in a subordinate position and size to the annual price." [S24]
- A free trial's length and the price charged once it ends must be clearly stated before purchase: "Clearly indicate how long the free trial lasts and the price billed." [S24]

### Google Play rules

- Google Play developers must clearly and explicitly disclose offer terms, subscription cost, and billing-cycle frequency: "clearly and explicitly disclosing your offer terms, the cost of your subscription." [S25]
- Developers must not mislead users about any subscription service or content offered in the app: "must not mislead users about any subscription services or content." [S25]
- Before enrolling a user in a subscription, the app must clearly and accurately describe the offer's duration, pricing, and what content/service is included: "clearly and accurately describe the terms of your offer." [S25]
- A subscription's name must not misrepresent the offer, e.g. it cannot be called "Free Trial": "don't name your subscription 'Free Trial'." [S26]
- Subscription/offer terms must be visible to the user without an extra tap or action to reveal them: "Users should not have to perform any additional action to review the information." [S26]

### US state law & FTC dark-pattern rules

- California requires that automatic-renewal or continuous-service terms be shown clearly and conspicuously before the deal is completed: "in a clear and conspicuous manner." [S27]
- Those renewal terms must sit visually right next to (or, for voice offers, immediately before) the button/action the consumer uses to consent: "in visual proximity to the request for consent to the offer." [S27]
- A business must get the consumer's affirmative consent to the auto-renewal terms before charging their card: "without first obtaining the consumer's affirmative consent to the agreement." [S27]
- The FTC's 2024 "click-to-cancel" negative-option rule has been struck down in full and is NOT currently in force: "we grant the petitions for review and vacate the Rule" (Custom Communications, Inc. v. FTC, 8th Cir., July 2025). [S28]
- The Eighth Circuit vacated the rule only for a procedural defect (no preliminary regulatory analysis), not on the underlying consumer-protection merits: "the procedural deficiencies of the Commission's rulemaking process are fatal here." [S28]
- As of the current cycle the FTC is redoing this rulemaking from scratch rather than the vacated rule being in effect: "issuing an advance notice of proposed rulemaking." [S28]
- FTC staff have flagged countdown timers implying a purchase deadline as a recognized dark pattern: "countdown timers designed to make consumers believe they only have a limited time." [S29]
- FTC staff have flagged deliberately hard-to-find or multi-step cancellation flows as a dark pattern: "made it extremely difficult to cancel free trials and subscription plans." [S29]
- The FTC has brought and won a live enforcement case against burying the "skip Prime" option and gating cancellation behind a maze of retention offers: "Consumers had to first locate the cancellation flow, which Amazon made difficult" (FTC v. Amazon, 2023). [S30]
- Presenting several discounted "stay" offers before letting the consumer complete cancellation was itself part of the deceptive design the FTC challenged: "redirected to multiple pages that presented several offers to continue the subscription." [S30]
- The checkout button itself failing to clearly disclose that clicking it also enrolls the consumer in a paid subscription was found deceptive: the button "did not clearly state" that choosing it also meant agreeing to join Prime. [S30]
